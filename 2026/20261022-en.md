# On the Responsibility of Early Security Patch Disclosure in Open Source Software — A Proposal to Extend the FIRST Vulnerability Coordination Guidelines

## 1. Protecting Your Own Customers While Putting Others at Risk?

While examining the series of events surrounding the critical PHP vulnerability known as "PHP2Shell," I came across an issue that deserves closer attention.

In October 2026, when security updates for PHP were being prepared for release, multiple operating system vendors and container image providers were expected to publish their fixes in a coordinated manner.

However, information about the vulnerability leaked before the scheduled release, and Chainguard subsequently published its patched version approximately six hours ahead of the agreed disclosure time.

The problem here is that once the patched code becomes publicly available, attackers can also analyze the differences between the vulnerable and corrected versions.

Which functions were modified? Under what conditions does an invalid state arise? What additional checks were introduced?

Such information is valuable to defenders, but it can be equally valuable to attackers. In some cases, analyzing a security patch is sufficient to develop a working exploit.

As a result, customers of the vendor that releases its patch early become protected, while users of other distributions may face an increased risk of attack before patches are available to them.

I believe there is an important distinction to be made here.

**Providing superior security services exclusively to one's own customers is perfectly legitimate. However, making it easier to attack people who are not one's customers in the course of providing those services is an entirely different matter.**

Should we not make this distinction more explicit when defining the responsibilities of software distributors?

## 2. FIRST Already Has Guidelines for Multi-Party Vulnerability Coordination

In looking into this issue, I found that the Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams (FIRST) published the following guidelines in 2020:

[Guidelines and Practices for Multi-Party Vulnerability Coordination and Disclosure — Version 1.1](https://www.first.org/global/sigs/vulnerability-coordination/multiparty/guidelines-v1-1)

Traditionally, vulnerability reporting has often been understood as a process involving two parties: the person who discovers the vulnerability and the developer responsible for the affected software.

Modern software, however, involves complex dependency relationships. A vulnerability in a single library may affect numerous operating systems, applications, and services.

The FIRST guidelines address precisely these situations, in which multiple organizations are involved.

The participants include researchers who discover vulnerabilities (Finders), the original developers (Upstream Vendors), companies that incorporate and distribute the affected software (Downstream Vendors), other vendors using the same technology (Peer Vendors), and organizations that coordinate communication among the parties (Coordinators).

One particularly interesting point is that the guidelines explicitly recognize that publishing a fix may reveal vulnerabilities in products maintained by other vendors.

> Vendors should be aware that publication of fixes can disclose the presence of the same vulnerability in other vendors’ components.

In other words, publishing a security fix may also reveal that other vendors' products remain vulnerable to the same underlying problem.

Furthermore, Variant 8 of Use Case 2 addresses a situation in which a vulnerability in an open-source library is reported to one vendor but also affects others. One of the suggested responses is for the vendor receiving the report to notify the upstream maintainer.

Thus, the issue discussed here is already within the scope of the FIRST guidelines.

However, these provisions primarily describe recommended coordination practices. They do not explicitly establish the responsibility of a distributor to avoid increasing the risks faced by third parties through premature disclosure of a security patch.

I would therefore like to propose the following additional principle.

## 3. Proposal: Distributors Have a Responsibility Not to Unnecessarily Increase Risks to Third Parties

The central principle of this proposal is:

**Software distributors should not unnecessarily facilitate attacks against other systems affected by the same vulnerability through actions taken to protect their own customers.**

The intended subjects of this principle are not ordinary end users.

Rather, it applies to organizations that distribute software to third parties, such as operating system distributors, redistributors of language runtimes and libraries, and container image providers.

More specifically, I propose adding the following principles.

### Principle 1: Report Discovered Vulnerabilities Upstream

When a downstream distributor discovers an undisclosed vulnerability originating in an upstream software component, it should confidentially report the issue to the upstream maintainer within a reasonable period.

This does not necessarily mean leaving the responsibility for fixing the problem entirely to the upstream developer.

The downstream distributor may independently develop a fix or prepare mitigations and validation procedures for its own customers.

However, when a vulnerability is discovered in shared upstream software, that knowledge should also be returned to the upstream project.

### Principle 2: Contribute Independent Fixes Upstream

When a downstream distributor develops an independent security patch, it should provide the relevant technical information and, where feasible, the patch itself to the upstream maintainer.

The upstream project may face different design constraints, and it may not be possible to adopt the downstream patch without modification.

Nevertheless, sharing knowledge about how the vulnerability can be addressed has substantial value.

This is particularly important for open-source projects maintained by relatively small teams.

### Principle 3: Coordinate Early Disclosure to Avoid Increasing Risks to Third Parties

This is the principle I particularly wish to emphasize.

Before publicly releasing a fix for an undisclosed vulnerability, a downstream distributor should assess whether the release could make it easier to attack third-party systems that remain unpatched.

If such risks are reasonably foreseeable, the distributor should coordinate the timing of disclosure with the upstream maintainer and other affected distributors.

**The mere fact that a vendor has completed its own patch should not, by itself, be considered sufficient justification for determining the disclosure date.**

### Principle 4: Do Not Prohibit Early Remediation for Customers

An important clarification is necessary here.

There is no need to prohibit vendors from providing patches to paying customers ahead of the general release.

For example, a vendor may confidentially provide patched binaries to a limited group of customers while sharing relevant information with the upstream maintainer.

However, distributing binaries is not inherently safe. Attackers may be able to infer the vulnerability by comparing patched and unpatched binaries. Even when distribution is limited to customers, the possibility of information leakage must be considered.

The issue is not whether customers are protected, but whether the method used to protect them unnecessarily increases risks for others.

Furthermore, it would be inappropriate to prohibit disclosure indefinitely when the upstream project has failed to respond for an extended period or when exploitation is already widespread.

A reasonable coordination period should be established, after which necessary disclosure or the publication of protective measures may be justified.

## 4. Proposed Addition to the FIRST Guidelines

The principles described above could be incorporated into the FIRST guidelines through an additional provision along the following lines.

### Proposed Additional Principle: Responsibility of Downstream Distributors to Avoid Increasing Third-Party Exposure

*Downstream vendors and distributors should take reasonable steps to ensure that their vulnerability remediation and disclosure activities do not unnecessarily increase the risk of exploitation for other affected parties.*

*In particular:*

1. *A downstream vendor that discovers a vulnerability in a shared upstream component should report the vulnerability confidentially to the upstream maintainer or an appropriate coordinator within a reasonable timeframe.*

2. *A downstream vendor that develops an independent security fix should share relevant technical information and, where feasible, the fix itself with the upstream maintainer through a trusted channel.*

3. *Before publicly distributing a security fix for a vulnerability that remains unpatched in other affected products, the vendor should assess whether the disclosure of code changes, binaries, or technical details could materially facilitate exploitation of those products.*

4. *Where such risks are reasonably foreseeable, the vendor should coordinate the timing and manner of disclosure with affected upstream and peer vendors, or an appropriate coordinator.*

5. *This principle should not prevent confidential remediation for customers, nor should it require indefinite withholding of fixes when coordination fails, exploitation is ongoing, or continued nondisclosure would cause greater harm.*

Put simply, the principle is this:

**"Vendors are free to protect their own customers. However, if doing so creates additional risks for other vendors' customers, they also have a responsibility to mitigate those risks."**

## 5. Why Start with Guidelines Rather Than License Requirements?

My initial thought was to incorporate vulnerability reporting and upstream contribution obligations directly into open-source software licenses.

However, introducing new obligations into software licenses raises questions about compatibility with existing open-source licenses and compliance with the Open Source Definition maintained by the Open Source Initiative (OSI).

Security decisions also frequently involve exceptional circumstances.

For example, delaying the release of a patch may sometimes increase the damage caused by a vulnerability. The upstream maintainer may be unresponsive, or the affected parties may disagree about the appropriate disclosure schedule.

Applying uniform legal obligations to such situations would be difficult.

Moreover, if vulnerability researchers become reluctant to report their findings because they fear legal consequences, the result would be counterproductive.

For these reasons, I believe a more practical first step would be to extend existing guidelines, such as those developed by FIRST, to explicitly address the responsibilities of downstream distributors.

Operating system vendors, container image providers, and other distributors could then adopt these principles as part of their own vulnerability response policies.

Eventually, the principles could be incorporated into industry certification programs, procurement requirements, or contractual agreements.

## 6. Good Intentions in Publishing Security Patches Are Not Enough

In open-source software development, discovering vulnerabilities, fixing them, and publishing the fixes are generally considered desirable activities.

That understanding should certainly be preserved.

However, in today's software ecosystem, where the same component may be incorporated into numerous products, a disclosure that benefits one organization may expose users of other organizations to substantial additional risks.

Attackers do not need to attack products that have already been patched. They can instead analyze publicly released fixes and search for products that remain vulnerable.

Even when a security patch is published with the best intentions, its consequences extend beyond the organization that publishes it.

This is why vulnerability coordination involving multiple distributors requires consideration not only of whether an organization has handled its own responsibilities correctly, but also of how its actions affect the risks faced by others.

Commercial operating system vendors and software distributors, in particular, should arguably recognize some responsibility toward the security of the shared software infrastructure on which their products and businesses depend, in addition to their obligations to their own customers.

**Providing better services to paying customers is a legitimate form of competition. But there is no need for that competition to take a form that makes it easier to attack those who are not customers.**

Making this distinction explicit is the central purpose of this proposal.

The FIRST guidelines for multi-party vulnerability coordination already address many aspects of this problem.

By adding the principle that **distributors should not unnecessarily increase the risks faced by third parties**, we may be able to establish a clearer framework of responsibility within today's open-source software ecosystem.
